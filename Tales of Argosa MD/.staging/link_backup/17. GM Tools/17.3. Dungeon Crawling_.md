When the PCs first enter a dungeon, there's a few things the GM and party should consider.

### Marching Order

First up, the party should declare their general **Marching Order:** who's in front, who's in the second rank, in the middle, and who's taking up the rear. In playtesting we often lined up miniatures on the table as a visual reminder.

This information is important for the GM to determine who is likely to see what first, who might be at risk of triggering a trap, who might be targeted by an enemy sneaking up behind them, who might be positioned to attempt a rescue, and so on. When uncertain as to who might be the target of an attack, a **random d6 roll**, nominating PCs by Marching Order (or clockwise) works well.

> [!note] Readied Gear
> The gear that the adventurers have **in their hands** can be a relevant consideration if the party is taken by surprise, for example when triggering a trap or being ambushed by hidden foes. Having a free hand to grab a falling comrade, or a shield to deflect an arrow trap, could be important.

### Who's Got the Light?

Speaking of readied gear, the most important question is who's carrying the light? Players should **nominate** which adventurers have **light sources** p.55 and whether they are torches, lanterns, etc. Hirelings are an excellent option as torch bearers, freeing up PC hands for weapons, shields, and so on. Keeping them protected within or behind the party will help shore up their Morale in battle.

### Dead Adventurers

Sometimes **PCs die**. When this happens in a town or the wilds, it's easy for the player to make a new PC that meets the party in the next tavern or mountain pass. Death in the **middle** of a dungeon however can be a bit trickier, especially if it happens at the start of a session. The player could play a **hireling** (if the party has some), or take over the **monsters** for a while. But if they're keen to roll up a new PC, **roll 1d6** for an **instant** introduction!

### Dungeon Events

To ensure meaningful choices while exploring, ToA uses **Dungeon Events**, which are triggered by an escalating **Dungeon Tally** ("DT"). The party's **DT starts at zero.** As they explore, moving through corridors, searching rooms, disarming traps, and so on, the GM **adds 1** to the DT and updates the players with the total. A giant d6 in the middle of the table works well as a visual tracker. Actions that are **loud** or otherwise **prone to draw attention** might add extra points at the GM's option.

**How much the PCs can do** before the DT increases is decided by the GM on a case by case basis. Those familiar with old school games might employ the classic ten minute dungeon turn.

When the **DT reaches 6**, **roll 2d6** on the **Dungeon Event** table. The GM might also roll when:

1. The PCs **draw attention** to themselves,
2. They are being **overly indecisive**, or
3. There is a **natural lull** in the adventure.

As always, GM discretion applies: as referee you may simply decide that something happens (eg a Random Encounter occurs, etc). After determining the Dungeon Event, **reset the DT to zero.**

#### Consult the Bones

For a simpler option, ***Consult the Bones*** (p.212) when (1), (2), or (3) arises to determine whether a **Dungeon Event** occurs (if so, **roll 2d6** on the table). Apply *Likely* or *Unlikely* as desired. *Fortune* and *Misfortune* results might assist in interpreting entries for clues, warning signs, possible allies, etc.

> [!note] Designer's Thoughts
> Note that Dungeon Events are not necessarily adverse to the party; they just mean that something interesting happens. Helpful clues and even allies might be found, especially when using monster **Reaction** and **Activity** rolls (p.66).
>
> For quieter complexes or very small parties, the GM might use a DT trigger of 8 or 10. For very dangerous locations, or large parties with several hirelings, 3, 4 or 5 may be more appropriate. **Custom entries** in your **Random Encounter tables** are a terrific way to breathe life into your dungeons, and also allow you to set ballpark danger levels.
